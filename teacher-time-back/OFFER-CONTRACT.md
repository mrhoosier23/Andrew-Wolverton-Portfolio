# Safe AI Assistant Lab: Offer Contract

## The problem

Teachers repeatedly rebuild directions, family updates, meeting follow-up, planning structures, and resource organization. AI may help, but an unguided tool demo adds another thing to learn and leaves safety decisions unclear.

## The promise

Bring one recurring task. Leave with one reusable, school-aligned assistant blueprint that can produce a useful first draft without student records. The teacher reviews every result and keeps every professional decision.

This is a build lab, not a keynote, product demo, compliance certification, or prompt library.

## What the school provides

Before the lab, a school leader completes a 45-minute setup conversation and provides:

- the approved AI tool and account teachers may use;
- the school's current AI, privacy, acceptable-use, and data rules;
- one escalation contact for policy or regulated questions;
- a cohort of 8-20 educators with laptops and access to the approved environment;
- a room with display capability or a reliable video-conference environment.

If no approved tool is available, teachers complete the blueprint and fictional practice in paper/demo mode. Andrew does not recommend bypassing approval.

## What teachers bring

- A laptop.
- Access to the school-approved AI environment, if one exists.
- One recurring task they personally perform.
- Optional teacher-created, non-sensitive material they are authorized to reuse.

Teachers do not bring student records, student work, grades, rosters, IEP or 504 information, behavior or attendance records, family records, personnel matters, or confidential school records.

## What teachers learn

By the end of the 90-minute lab, teachers can:

1. Choose a task appropriate for an assistant.
2. Recognize direct and indirect identifiers.
3. Separate approved, pause-and-check, and never-enter inputs.
4. Define the assistant's role, source boundaries, output, refusal behavior, and human decision line.
5. Test with fictional or student-neutral material.
6. Review an output for accuracy, bias, appropriateness, and alignment.
7. Measure whether the assistant saves time without creating new correction work.

## What each teacher leaves with

- one-task assistant brief;
- safe-input map;
- copy-ready assistant instructions;
- refusal rule;
- fictional test;
- four-point review card;
- two-week measurement log;
- a clear keep, revise, or stop decision.

## Delivery sequence

### Before: 45-minute leadership setup

Andrew maps approved tools, permitted use, data boundaries, likely task lanes, and escalation ownership. Leadership receives the setup guide and a customized opening slide.

### During: 90-minute teacher build lab

- 0-10: Name the time drain and see a completed example.
- 10-25: Confirm the approved environment and protect inputs.
- 25-40: Choose one bounded task and define success.
- 40-65: Build the assistant blueprint.
- 65-80: Test with the fictional practice pack.
- 80-90: Review, save, and start the two-week measurement.

### After: 30-minute virtual follow-up

Two weeks later, the cohort compares measured time, usefulness, corrections, and safety slips. Each teacher chooses keep, revise, or stop. Andrew gives leadership a concise learning summary, not individual performance evaluation.

## Evidence used honestly

- Gallup reported that weekly teacher AI users estimated 5.9 hours saved per week in a 2025 national survey. This is self-reported and not a promise of results.
- Gallup reported in 2026 that only 18% of surveyed public K-12 teachers had received formal guidance from administrators on AI use.
- NYC Public Schools requires ERMA approval before AI tools use student data and requires human oversight and critical review of outputs.
- FERPA's definition of personally identifiable information includes direct identifiers, indirect identifiers, and combinations of details that can identify a student.
