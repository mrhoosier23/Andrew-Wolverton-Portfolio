# Teacher Time-Back Lab: Complete Presentation Script

## The promise

Teachers do not come to this session to master AI. They come to stop rebuilding one recurring piece of work.

By the end of the lab, each teacher has:

- one carefully bounded assistant blueprint;
- one list of allowed and prohibited inputs;
- one fictional practice test;
- one human review routine;
- one two-week keep, revise, or stop measurement plan.

The finished product is not a vague prompt and not an autonomous classroom tool. It is a reusable set of instructions for one task, used only in the school's approved AI environment, with the teacher making every final decision.

## Format at a glance

1. Leadership setup: 45 minutes before the teacher session.
2. Teacher build lab: 90 minutes.
3. Measurement period: two weeks.
4. Cohort follow-up: 30 minutes.

## What must be ready before the teacher lab

- The school has named the exact approved AI product, account type, and sign-in route.
- A leader has named what teachers may and may not enter.
- A school contact is available for policy, privacy, technology, and regulated questions.
- The Teacher Workbook, Completed Reuse Planner Example, Fictional Practice Pack, and Safe Input and Review Cards are ready.
- The approved tool has been tested on the room network.
- Paper/demo mode is ready if access fails or no approved environment has been named.

No participant needs coding experience, prompting vocabulary, an existing assistant, student records, or a completed lesson plan.

## 45-minute leadership setup script

### 0:00-0:05 | Establish the decision

Say:

> This is not a policy-writing meeting and it is not a product sales demo. We are creating a safe container for one beginner workshop. Teachers need to know the exact approved environment, the input boundaries, the human decision line, and who answers questions that sit outside this lab.

Confirm the cohort, date, format, and school contact.

### 0:05-0:15 | Name the approved environment

Ask:

- What exact product may participants use?
- What account or tenant must they use?
- How do they confirm that they are in the approved environment?
- Are there role, age, feature, sharing, retention, or data-processing restrictions?
- Who owns the answer when a participant is unsure?

Decision: write the exact tool and account route on the opening slide. If leadership cannot name them, use paper/demo mode only.

### 0:15-0:25 | Set the input lanes

Walk through the green, yellow, and red cards.

Say:

> Tool approval and task approval are different questions. An approved account does not make every input or every use appropriate.

Confirm examples that fit the school's local rules. The lab itself uses blank, public, fictional, educator-owned, or approved student-neutral material. It does not need student records.

### 0:25-0:35 | Draw the human decision line

Confirm that an assistant may draft, organize, reformat, or propose options. It does not grade, score, rank, diagnose, determine accommodations, recommend discipline, make placement or safety decisions, send communications, or replace required professional review.

Name the four review questions: accuracy, bias, appropriateness, and alignment.

### 0:35-0:42 | Choose the first-build lanes

Select two or three lanes that match the cohort:

- Reuse Planner
- Family Message Drafter for neutral logistics
- Meeting-to-Action Assistant for approved neutral notes
- Teaching Library Organizer
- Feedback Framework Builder without student scoring

Choose tasks teachers can verify quickly and perform without student records.

### 0:42-0:45 | Confirm day-of ownership

Confirm:

- who opens the session;
- who answers local policy questions;
- who handles sign-in problems;
- whether devices and printed materials are ready;
- where unresolved questions are captured;
- how the two-week follow-up will be scheduled.

## 90-minute teacher lab script

### 0:00-0:05 | Welcome and lower the entry point

On screen: the promise and one finished Reuse Planner example.

Say:

> You are not here to master AI. You are here to stop rebuilding one piece of work. You need one repeated task, the school's approved environment, and your professional judgment. I will give you the structure.

> We will not use student records today. We will build with blank, fictional, public, educator-owned, or school-approved student-neutral material.

Ask participants to complete this sentence in the workbook:

> I keep rebuilding, rewriting, reorganizing, or carrying home...

Completion check: everyone has named one repeated workload, even if it is still broad.

### 0:05-0:12 | Show the finished product first

Open the Completed Reuse Planner Example. Point to six visible fields:

1. one job;
2. allowed sources;
3. exact output;
4. quality criteria;
5. refusal behavior;
6. the teacher's final decision.

Say:

> This is what you are leaving with. It is more durable than a clever one-time prompt because it tells the assistant what job it has, what it may use, where it must stop, and what only you can decide.

Show one fictional input and the expected output shape. Do not perform a long AI demo.

### 0:12-0:25 | Step 1: Choose one task

Website state: only the Choose slide is visible.

Say:

> The first assistant should be boring in the best possible way. It should handle a repeated, bounded task that you can check quickly.

Apply four filters:

- Repeatable: does it happen often enough to be worth setting up?
- Student-neutral: can it work without student records or identifiable work?
- Reviewable: can you quickly tell whether the result is usable?
- Bounded: can the job fit in one sentence?

Participant action:

1. List up to three time drains.
2. Circle the one that best passes all four filters.
3. Estimate how often it happens and how many minutes it currently takes.
4. Write what the saved time would be for.

Facilitator prompt:

> If your task needs a student's history, grade, disability information, behavior, or other record to work, it is not our first build. Narrow it or choose another task.

Completion check: each participant has one task stated as a single job.

### 0:25-0:40 | Step 2: Protect the inputs

Website state: only the Protect slide is visible.

Say:

> Before we write instructions, we decide what is allowed through the door. A name is not the only identifier. A rare combination of dates, places, roles, images, family details, or events can point to a person.

Introduce the green, yellow, and red lanes. Run the identifier scan:

- Who is named or could be recognized?
- What number, date, place, image, voice, or detail combination could point to a person?
- Does this task need student information at all?
- Can blank, public, fictional, teacher-owned, or approved student-neutral material do the job?

Participant action:

1. Name the exact school-approved tool and account.
2. List the sources the assistant may use.
3. List the categories it must never use.
4. Name the person or office to ask when unsure.

If the approved environment is unknown, say:

> Stay in blueprint mode. You can finish the design on paper without opening a personal workaround account.

Completion check: every blueprint has both an allowed-input list and a never-enter list.

### 0:40-0:65 | Step 3: Build the assistant blueprint

Website state: only the Build slide is visible.

Say:

> We are not chasing a magic phrase. We are writing a visible contract for one job.

Participants complete six fields:

1. Role: What one job does the assistant perform?
2. Sources: What may it use?
3. Output: What exact structure should it return?
4. Quality: What makes the draft useful?
5. Refusal: What must it stop and flag?
6. Human decision: What remains with the teacher?

Use this spoken build pattern:

> You are my [assistant name]. Your only job is to [one bounded task]. Use only [approved source types]. Return [exact output structure]. A useful result must [quality criteria]. If the request contains [prohibited categories] or asks you to [prohibited decisions], stop without repeating the information and ask for a safe replacement. You may draft or organize. I decide [human decision]. Remind me to review accuracy, bias, appropriateness, and alignment before use.

Facilitator coaching questions:

- Could a stranger tell exactly what this assistant does?
- Is the output easy to scan and verify?
- Does the refusal describe what to do next?
- Is the teacher's decision line explicit?
- Does any sentence quietly invite student data?

Completion check: each participant has a copy-ready instruction block.

### 0:65-0:80 | Step 4: Practice safely

Website state: only the Practice slide is visible. Distribute or open the Fictional Practice Pack now.

Say:

> We test the structure before we trust the workflow. Today the content is fictional on purpose.

Participant action:

1. Run or rehearse the Harbor City scenario.
2. Compare the output with the source pack.
3. Locate the planted unsupported bus-stop claim.
4. Check accuracy, bias, appropriateness, and alignment.
5. Revise one instruction based on the failure.

If the school-approved product is unavailable, pairs role-play the assistant using the same source pack and blueprint.

Completion check: every participant identifies at least one correction and improves one instruction.

### 0:80-0:88 | Step 5: Measure a real result

Website state: only the Measure slide is visible.

Say:

> Time saved is not the time it takes to generate a draft. It is the full task time after checking and correcting the output.

Participants create a two-week test:

- current minutes without the assistant;
- minutes with the assistant;
- correction minutes;
- usefulness rating;
- safety slips or unclear moments;
- final decision: keep, revise, or stop.

Decision rule:

- Keep if it is safe, useful, and reduces total work.
- Revise if the job is useful but the instructions or output need correction.
- Stop if it creates more work, cannot stay inside the approved lane, or is unreliable for the task.

### 0:88-0:90 | Close

Say:

> The assistant drafts. You decide. You now have one job, one safe source lane, one refusal line, one fictional test, one review routine, and one way to decide whether this is worth keeping.

Collect only cohort-level follow-up commitments. Do not collect student data or use the lab to evaluate teachers.

## Interactive safety rehearsal script

This is a four-question guided rehearsal, not a survey and not a high-stakes quiz. The interface should show one question at a time. A selected answer gives immediate plain-language feedback. The correct choice reveals the safe visual state and unlocks the next question. Wrong choices should remain neutral, not alarming or punitive.

### Question 1: Account check

Prompt: A teacher is ready to start. What comes first?

Correct response: Confirm the exact school-approved tool and account.

Reveal: the approved product, account type, and leadership contact are visibly checked.

Teaching point: A personal account is not a workaround when the approved environment is unknown.

### Question 2: Identifier check

Prompt: The name is removed, but rare details remain. What now?

Correct response: Stop and replace the material with fictional or student-neutral content.

Reveal: the identifying detail cloud is replaced by a fictional practice card.

Teaching point: indirect identifiers and combinations can identify someone.

### Question 3: Task check

Prompt: A teacher wants AI to grade identifiable student essays. What is the safer first build?

Correct response: Build a feedback-framework assistant using criteria and fictional examples.

Reveal: the automated grading task is replaced by a teacher-reviewed framework task.

Teaching point: begin with a lower-risk drafting aid, not a student-level decision.

### Question 4: Output check

Prompt: The draft looks polished. What happens before use?

Correct response: Review accuracy, bias, appropriateness, and alignment.

Reveal: the unsupported statement is crossed out, a correction is shown, and the four review checks are marked.

Teaching point: polished formatting is not evidence that an output is correct.

## 30-minute follow-up script

### 0:00-0:05 | Reopen the decision

Say:

> This is not a celebration of usage. We are deciding what was genuinely useful, what needs revision, and what should stop.

### 0:05-0:15 | Compare the full task time

Participants compare current minutes, assisted minutes, and correction minutes. Ask what they did with any time recovered.

### 0:15-0:23 | Diagnose patterns

Sort problems into:

- unclear task;
- poor source boundaries;
- vague output format;
- invented or missing content;
- too much correction work;
- unclear local policy;
- wrong task for an assistant.

### 0:23-0:28 | Decide

Each participant chooses keep, revise, or stop and records one reason.

### 0:28-0:30 | Route unresolved needs

Leadership records cohort-level support and policy questions. Do not use the results for teacher evaluation.

## Common participant questions

### "I do not understand AI."

You do not need AI vocabulary. You need one repeated task, a safe source lane, and the ability to review the draft. The workbook supplies the structure.

### "Will this save me five or seven hours a week?"

No fixed saving is promised. You are testing one task for two weeks and measuring the total time, including corrections.

### "Can I use student work if I remove the name?"

Not in this lab. Names are only one type of identifier, and local rules may be stricter. Use the Fictional Practice Pack and route the real workflow to the school's responsible lead.

### "Which AI platform should I use?"

Use only the product and account your school has approved. If none has been named, complete the blueprint in paper/demo mode.

### "Can it grade for me?"

The first build may help structure a rubric or neutral feedback framework. Student-level evaluation and the final score remain with the teacher.

### "What if the result is bad?"

That is useful evidence. Identify whether the task, source, output format, or quality criteria were unclear. Revise once. Stop if the workflow remains unreliable or creates more work.

## Presenter boundaries

Do not claim that:

- a tool or workflow is compliant, secure, legal, or locally approved;
- the lab replaces policy, privacy, legal, cybersecurity, special-education, labor, safety, or procurement expertise;
- a teacher will save a guaranteed number of hours;
- Andrew is a New York State licensed teacher;
- AI output is correct because it is polished;
- using more AI is the measure of success.

The measure of success is one safe, understandable, genuinely useful workflow that the teacher can keep, revise, or stop with informed judgment.
