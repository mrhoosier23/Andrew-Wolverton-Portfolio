About Andrew

I have spent most of my life helping people make something together.

Sometimes that has meant directing a school production, producing professional work, building an alumni campaign, creating a music program, untangling a website, or founding an organization from the ground up.

Read the story View experience

The path here

Lafayette to Bloomington to Pittsburgh to New York.

The places changed. The question stayed familiar: what do people need in order to create, participate, and keep going?

Lafayette and Indiana

I learned to make ambitious work with real people, real limits, and an opening night.

I taught theater and choir, directed productions, managed programs, coached students, worked with families, found sponsors, and stayed close to community theater. Teaching made project management concrete. A plan only mattered if students understood it, families trusted it, collaborators could use it, and the show opened.

Indiana University and the Singing Hoosiers

I stayed because creative community should not end at graduation.

Indiana University and the Singing Hoosiers shaped my musicianship and my sense of belonging. I later served the Singing Hoosiers Alumni Council for more than a decade, beginning in communications, becoming board president, and continuing as President Emeritus. The work included newsletters, alumni engagement, fundraising campaigns, committee leadership, and helping a dispersed community remain connected.

Carnegie Mellon

I wanted to understand what keeps creative work alive after the idea.

At Carnegie Mellon, I earned a master’s degree in arts management and studied the financial, operational, fundraising, marketing, and audience questions behind artistic work. The program gave language and structure to things I had already experienced in classrooms and rehearsal rooms.

Professional producing and New York

I learned from institutions, producers, small teams, and unfinished systems.

My professional path has included associate producing work on a Kathleen Turner production connected to Davenport, development and marketing at Red Bull Theater, small-business and nonprofit work, digital production, audio editing, and live performance. Moving to New York made the range of the work more visible, but the through line stayed the same: clarify the goal, organize the people and information, and get the work in front of an audience.

Discovery Sound Garden

Eventually, I built the organization I wanted to see exist.

I founded Discovery Sound Garden to create welcoming pathways into musicianship, recording, performance, and creative community. That has required program development, board and governance work, fundraising, marketing, budgeting, partnerships, operations, and a willingness to keep testing what people actually need.

What I believe

The work should respect the people doing it and the people trying to enter it.

Adults deserve welcoming ways to learn music.

People should not have to pretend they never stepped away or arrive already confident.

Small arts organizations deserve clear, usable systems.

They should not need corporate-sized teams to communicate well or maintain their work.

Technology should remove confusion.

A new tool is only useful if the people responsible for it understand what to do next.

AI should support judgment, not replace it.

Research and drafting can be accelerated. Responsibility, source checking, taste, and approval remain human.

Community arts can be accessible and ambitious.

Welcoming people in does not require lowering the artistic standard.

Creative work needs structures that fit real lives.

Systems should account for energy, time, learning differences, and the actual capacity of a team.
